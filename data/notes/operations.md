# data/operations.json

Operations — non-combat missions (the Jobs board in the War Room). WHY THIS EXISTS: before it, every route to money and to new fauna ran through winning battles. Held nodes paid income, battles paid purses, and `unlocksFauna` gated the Mail-Order catalog, so a player who lost had $22/day, a catalog containing exactly two species (Goat and Ram), and NO path to Water or Air anatomy — which is what the class triangle says you need in order to stop losing. That is a spiral with no floor, and this is the floor. Rules the set is built around: (1) at least one job must ALWAYS be runnable — no held nodes, no notoriety, no chimera, no anatomy; (2) failure never costs a creature, only time and a bruise, because a losing player cannot be punished for trying to stop losing; (3) `demands` IMPROVE the odds, they never gate the job — asking for Aquatic anatomy before you can rob the aquarium that would give you Aquatic anatomy is the same circle again; (4) heat is the price: every job raises notoriety, which is what makes the world harder, so this is a trade rather than free money. Conquest stays the better deal — it puts a species in the catalog to buy instantly and repeatedly, where a job hands you one animal, sometimes, on a timer, with the police getting interested.

## tuning

HEAT is what stops this being a printing press, and it is deliberately a mechanic rather than a nerf: trimming payouts would punish the broke player this whole board exists for, while heat only ever bites the player running jobs back to back. Every job adds heatPerJob + heatPerNotoriety x its notoriety — the FLAT part is the important half, because it means frequency itself costs, not just ambition. Decay is EXPONENTIAL (heatHalfLifeHours), not linear: linear decay is bang-bang, since heat either drains to zero or pins at the cap with nothing in between, whereas a half-life gives a smooth equilibrium that scales with how hard you are pushing. Measured: a once-a-day player launches at about 4 heat (odds essentially untouched), a player checking in three times a day at about 25, and one cycling jobs all evening at about 50 — a fifth off every success roll until they let the county calm down.

A JOB NEEDS A CREW, so the board runs as wide as your stable does (A4). It used to be one job at a time, full stop, which meant a session was: check timers, launch the one job, fight once, wait — and if the fight was lost, there was nothing left but four different ways to spend money. Slots scale with the creatures who are fit to work, and are capped so a large stable does not turn the board into an idle-game payout tap. Growing a stable was already the answer to A1's solo cliff; this makes it the answer to the empty evening as well.

## operations[]

R116 — `contract: true` SPLITS THE SET, and which side a job is on is a field here rather than a rule in the engine. The board is what you WORK: a charge, a creature, a decision about which of them goes where. A contract is what you ARRANGE — one at a time, auto-renewing, paid from `paidThrough` on the elapsed clock, asking nothing of the roster.

The split is the cure for a defect the charge bucket created. One shared scarce charge makes a lower-purse job strictly dominated: measured, the petting zoo, the feed co-op and the county fair ran 0, 2 and 6 times in a 180-day campaign, and the same at day 20, so it was not an early-game argument either. The three jobs that lost were precisely the three that need nobody carried anywhere — the set that did not want to be a tap in the first place. A4's floor survives it, and is strictly kinder for it: a lab with no territory, no money and no creatures still has a way to earn, and it signs rather than taps.

PRICING THE CHARGE WAS TRIED IN BOTH DIRECTIONS AND REJECTED IN BOTH, and the record is here because each failure looks like success from one end of the board. A FLAT cost plus one shared charge makes expected value per charge just chance x purse, so the fattest job wins every time: over 180 days the walker ran the aquarium 162 times, the reptile house 148, and the petting zoo, feed co-op and county fair a combined EIGHT. The cheap end went dark and the wire went with it — the loudest line in the county doubled from 1.55% to 3.25%, which is the metronome rebuilt at the other end of the board. Pricing the charge BY the purse inverts it exactly: the cheap jobs then take 1,079 of 1,081 launches. The answer was not a price at all.

A retainer files ONE LEDGER LINE A DAY, and that turned out to be load-bearing for R109 rather than decoration: taking the three quiet jobs off the board silenced their headline pools completely and the wire fell from 411 distinct phrasings to 381, under R109's floor of 400. The county stopped noticing the missing goats because nobody was driving out to fetch them. The line is DAY-stamped rather than tick-stamped, so a player who opens the app six times an hour hears the arrangement once and a player who was away a week hears it as the week it was. It alternates two pools on purpose: the job's own headline keeps the county noticing the goats, and `op_contract` is the ledger line, which is a different KIND of sentence — a heist is an event, a retainer is a line item, and one pool cannot be both without the tone gate noticing.
